# 1. What is an LLM, really?

You have probably used ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude. Behind each of them there is an LLM, a "Large Language
Model". An LLM is a kind of neural network: a huge computer program made of billions of small numbers
that are tuned during training. Many people say that neural networks work a bit like the human brain,
because the idea was first inspired by how brain cells connect to each other.

LLMs are already very impressive. They write code, summarize long documents, pass difficult exams and
even help mathematicians with proofs. Because of this, many people believe that if we just make these
models bigger and train them on more data, one day they will reach AGI (Artificial General Intelligence):
an AI that can think as well as a human in almost everything.


# 2. The big question: can AI make a real scientific breakthrough?

Writing code and passing exams is one thing. But can an LLM do what the greatest human scientists did?
Can it make a real breakthrough, like Einstein did with his theory of relativity?

Here is a simple way to put the question: imagine we give a modern LLM all the knowledge that Einstein
had in his time. Could it invent his theory by itself?

To answer this, we first need to understand how scientists think. So let's take a small step back.


# 3. Three ways of thinking

More than a hundred years ago, the American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce said that there are three
basic ways to reason:

- Deduction: you start from a general rule and apply it to a specific case.
- Induction: you look at many specific examples and find a general rule.
- Abduction: you see something surprising and you make the best guess to explain it.

The first two are the "normal" tools of science, and computers are already good at them. The third one
is the creative one. As we will see, the third one is the key to our question. Let's look at each one
with examples.


# 4. Deduction: from a general rule to a specific case

Deduction goes from general to specific. You start with a rule that you accept as true, and you apply it
to one specific case. Here are two examples.

Example 1: The keycard building
Imagine a master rule that applies to an entire building.
- General rule: Every door in this building needs a keycard to open.
- Specific case: Room 402 is a door in this building.
- Conclusion: So, Room 402 needs a keycard to open. You know this 100% for certain, without ever testing
  the door.

Example 2: The triangle
In Euclidean geometry (the geometry we learn at school), we rely on established axioms and theorems.
- General rule: The interior angles of every triangle always add up to 180 degrees.
- Specific case: You draw a specific triangle where two angles are 60 and 50 degrees.
- Conclusion: So, the third angle must be 70 degrees (180 − 60 − 50).

Deduction is certain. Think about geometry again. In geometry we have axioms: basic rules that we do not
prove, but accept, such as "from two points, only one straight line can pass". Based on these axioms, we
prove that the sum of the angles of a triangle is 180 degrees. Then, based on this, we prove the famous
Pythagorean rule, a² + b² = c². Each step is built on the step before. This is a formal proof, and if the
process and the formulas are used correctly, the conclusion is 100% correct. That is why deduction has
full certainty: if the rules are true and you apply them correctly, the conclusion is true.

Where is AI here? AI is rapidly improving at deduction. Formal proof systems like Lean and Google
DeepMind's AlphaProof, which are mostly rule-based systems, can do step-by-step proofs, and nowadays
LLMs are also doing it. Many research papers we see nowadays are this kind of work (applying known rules
step by step), and LLMs already help researchers with it. In short: AI is rapidly improving at
deduction.


# 5. Induction: from many examples to a general rule

Induction goes the other way: from specific to general. You see a lot of smaller observations, and they
make you conclude a generalization. Here are three examples.

Example 1: The falling dominoes
Imagine an infinite line of dominoes standing upright.
- Observation 1: You push the first domino, and it falls.
- Observation 2: You notice that the dominoes are spaced so that any falling domino will always knock
  over the next one.
- Generalization: You conclude that every single domino in the entire line will fall, without having to
  watch each one individually.

Example 2: Adding odd numbers
Look at the sum of odd numbers, starting from 1:
  1 = 1 (which is 1²)
  1 + 3 = 4 (which is 2²)
  1 + 3 + 5 = 9 (which is 3²)
  1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 (which is 4²)
- Generalization: After seeing these smaller cases, you conclude that the sum of the first n odd numbers
  is always n².

Example 3: The bag of marbles
You have a bag full of marbles. You cannot see inside it, and you want to know what color the marbles
are.
- Observations: You pull out the first marble: it is red. You pull out a second one: red. You pull out ten
  more: all red.
- Generalization: Based on these repeated small observations, you conclude that all the marbles in the
  bag are red.
(Note: in everyday life, this kind of reasoning is probabilistic. In mathematics there is a special
method called "mathematical induction", which can prove a generalization like the dominoes or the odd
numbers with 100% certainty.)

Induction is not fully certain. Mathematical approaches can increase its certainty as much as possible,
but in nature we may always see a new observation that proves the previous rule wrong. Maybe the next
marble in the bag is blue.

Where is AI here? Induction is exactly how LLMs learn. During training, an LLM reads billions of
sentences and learns patterns from them: which word usually comes after which. This is induction at a
gigantic scale. In short: AI has mastered induction.


# 6. Deduction vs. induction at a glance

| | Deduction | Induction |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | general → specific | specific → general |
| Starts from | rules (axioms) | observations (data) |
| Example | Every door needs a keycard → Room 402 needs a keycard | 12 red marbles → all marbles are red |
| Certainty | 100%, if the rules are true and used correctly | not fully certain; probable |
| AI today | rapidly improving (Lean, AlphaProof, LLMs) | mastered (this is how LLMs learn) |


# 7. When the rules themselves change

Deduction and induction are powerful. But both have a limit: they work inside the rules we already
have. Deduction follows the rules. Induction finds patterns in data we already collected. Yet some of
the biggest moments in science happened when someone changed the rules themselves. Here are three
examples.

Geometry. In the school geometry of Euclid, "through two points, only one straight line can pass". But
now think about the surface of a ball, like planet Earth. Between the North Pole and the South Pole,
there is not one shortest path but infinitely many: every line of longitude is one. On a ball, the
angles of a triangle also add up to more than 180 degrees. This "non-Euclidean" geometry was not
discovered by following Euclid's rules more carefully. Someone had to dare to change a basic rule.
(Later, Einstein used exactly this kind of geometry in his theory of gravity.)

Mathematics. At school we learn that you cannot take the square root of a negative number. But
mathematicians decided to imagine a new number, called i, where i × i = −1. So the square root of −1 is
i (and also −i). These "imaginary numbers" sounded crazy at first, but today engineers use them every
day, for example in electricity and signal processing.

Physics. In everyday life, an object can only be in one place at a time. Your phone is either on the
table or in your pocket, never both. But in quantum physics, tiny particles like electrons and photons
can be in a "superposition". In one popular way of explaining it (the Copenhagen interpretation), the
particle does not have one fixed position until we measure it; in a sense, it is in several places at
once.

None of these ideas came from deduction, because deduction only follows the old rules. And none came
from induction, because no data at that time showed them. So how were they discovered?


# 8. Abduction: the creative jump

This is where the third way of thinking comes in: abduction. Abduction is just like guessing: you see
something surprising, and you make the best guess to explain it. The conclusion is a guess. Here are two
examples.

Example 1: The wet grass
- Surprise: The grass is wet this morning.
- Known rule: Rain makes grass wet.
- Best guess: It probably rained last night.
It could also have been a sprinkler, so abduction is a guess, not a proof. But it is the creative step:
it proposes an explanation that nobody gave you.

Example 2: Einstein and gravity
Einstein guessed something that nobody believed before: that gravity and acceleration might be the same
thing. From this guess, his famous theory of General Relativity was born. (We will look at this story
step by step in the next sections.)

In science, the biggest abductions are not small guesses about grass. They are new basic rules, like
"maybe space is curved" or "maybe energy comes in small packets". This creative leap is called "the
jump", and it is the thing that usually leads to the major breakthroughs in science.

Here is a simple picture to keep in mind for the rest of this article: the box of knowledge. Imagine
that all of human knowledge and science is a big box. Most areas of this box are already filled: humans
have discovered them. But some parts or points inside the box are still empty: things that humans have
missed. Using the existing rules and data (deduction and induction), we can fill those empty spaces
inside the box. But a jump is different. A jump is a breakthrough: it is thinking outside of the box,
and it increases the size of the box itself. Non-Euclidean geometry, imaginary numbers and quantum
physics from the previous section were all jumps like this: each of them made the box of knowledge
bigger.

And this is our research question: can LLMs do this jump or not?

To answer this, there are two research papers and one "hot take" (a blog post):
- Paper 1: "LLMs Can't Jump" by Tom Zahavy (Google DeepMind, 2026).
  https://openreview.net/forum?id=klU4737opt and https://www.tomzahavy.com/projects/llms-cant-jump
- Paper 2: "LLMs Don't Pay for the Jump" by Paras Balani and Subhrakanta Panda (2026), written as an answer
  to Paper 1. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14397 and https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14397v1
- Hot take: "Hot take: LLM can 'jump'" by Yong Zheng-Xin, a blog post which argues the opposite.
  https://yongzx.github.io/blog/2026/08/08/llm-can-jump

If scientific discovery were only induction plus deduction, then scaling LLMs (more compute and larger
models) would be enough. Both papers argue that it is not.

AI today: missing — "the jump".

Both papers agree that LLMs can't jump, but they differ in the reason WHY.


# 9. How a jump happens in humans like Einstein

How is a new theory born? Here are the steps:

1. We humans experience something.
2. We guess something that is not real yet (not part of our knowledge yet), using imagination. This is
   the jump.
3. The guess becomes an axiom: a new basic rule.
4. Based on the new axiom, using the same logic and process we applied before, we predict things. The
   logic is the same, but its base (the axioms) has changed. An axiom is like a base, a seed or a
   starting point. Logic derives predictions from the axiom.
5. We test our predictions with experiments. The theory stays accepted until an experiment or a test
   shows that it is wrong.

This is similar to how Einstein came to General Relativity:

1. Experience: He first had an experience: a person in an elevator. (We will discuss this elevator later,
   since this is not a physics article.)
2. The jump: Then, using his imagination, he guessed that maybe the world works in a way nobody had
   thought of before: maybe gravity and acceleration are the same thing. This jump cannot be done by AI.
3. The axiom: This guess became a basic rule of his new theory (the "equivalence principle"). This is
   axiom creation.
4. Predictions: Then he applied logic to it (with years of hard mathematics) and predicted that light
   bends near the Sun or near other enormous objects. This step, a statement or prediction derived from
   axioms, can also be done by AI.
5. The test: Finally, in 1919, scientists measured starlight passing near the Sun during a solar
   eclipse, and the prediction was true.


# 10. How could a jump happen in LLMs?

The problem here is that no one can hand over the jump (the creative imagination) to an LLM, because it
simply did not exist before.

LLMs are statistical models. LLM training can be oversimplified as curve fitting: there is a lot of
natural language text, and we train the "brain" (the neural network) to fit it. The training data is
like a square or a frame. During training, we try to maximize the area of this frame that the LLM
learns. The more of this frame's area it covers, the better.

This frame is our box of knowledge from Section 8. The text that humans have written covers most areas
of the box. Inside the box, LLMs can do a lot: using the existing rules and data, they can fill the empty
spaces and points that humans have missed. This is already very useful, and it is what LLMs are good at.

But a jump means stepping outside of this frame, or thinking outside of the box, and making the box
bigger. It means the LLM has to think about something that was not in its training data. In AI language, this is called "out of
distribution" (OOD). It is not clear, or proven, whether an LLM can step outside of this frame and think
outside of the box or not. But the authors of both papers argue that LLMs cannot do it. They give
different reasons WHY LLMs can't jump, and each of them proposes a different fix.


# 11. Paper 1: Zahavy's three reasons why LLMs can't jump

Tom Zahavy gives three arguments:

1. Induction sometimes fails.
2. Deduction starts from axioms and rules, and it cannot create them.
3. LLMs have no grounding in the physical world.

And then he proposes how to fix it. Let's go through them one by one.


# 12. Reason 1: induction sometimes fails (the Vulcan story)

Here is an example of induction failing. There was a topic in physics where, using Newtonian physics
(the one we learn at high school), we could predict the orbit of every planet, and all predictions came
out correct except one: a tiny extra drift in Mercury's orbit. Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun,
and its orbit drifted a little more than Newton's laws said it should.

Astronomers kept Newton's laws of gravity and assumed an invisible planet near the Sun, named "Vulcan",
whose gravity would explain the drift. In other words: using the existing laws, Mercury's orbit could not
be predicted correctly, so they added an exception, saying: "Maybe there is a planet that we have not
discovered yet, named Vulcan, and that's why the drift happens." They just introduced an exception, a
"patch", so that the whole set of axioms and observations would become correct again.

They did not rethink: "Maybe our understanding of gravity is wrong; maybe gravity is something else."
Why? Because using the existing laws they could predict everything almost perfectly, so the data gave
them no reason to change their rules, except for Mercury. So they did not find it necessary to change
anything, and instead they just "patched" the error with the assumption that a planet exists near the
Sun. This was wrong: such a planet was never discovered.

The interesting thing is that they had done the same patching trick before, with Neptune, and
surprisingly it worked. In the 1840s, the orbit of Uranus did not fit Newton's laws either. Astronomers
guessed that an unknown planet was pulling on it, calculated where it should be, and in 1846 they found
it: Neptune. So they thought the same patch would work one more time, and there was no reason to change
their laws or assumptions about gravity.

But Einstein was different on this topic. He "rethought" the laws of physics on gravity and invented
something new, which could perfectly describe the drift in Mercury's orbit WITHOUT THE NEED for any
PATCH! Einstein did not say: "OK, the existing rules work in 99.9% of the cases, so for the remaining
0.1%, let's add an exception." Instead, he was like: "Something about gravity does not fit together, and
I will not just patch it. Let's rethink what gravity is from the beginning." (Interestingly, his main
reason was not Mercury's drift. It was that Newton's gravity did not fit with his own earlier theory,
special relativity, and the elevator idea we will see later. In 1915, his new theory then explained
Mercury perfectly.) So Einstein decided to jump (abduction) rather than patching.

So after this example, talking about LLMs: will the induction of LLMs fail in the same way? Yes,
probably. LLMs can be simplified as curve-fitting learners: they learn to fit the training data. During
training, an LLM just tries to minimize its error, so it fixes or patches the exceptions. A learner that
only reduces its error on data always picks the smallest fix: the patch. Rewriting the whole theory is
never the smallest fix, so the data never asks for it. So if an LLM were in the same scenario as the
Mercury orbit drift, it would not rethink gravity like Einstein; it would patch it.

In our box picture: the Vulcan patch tried to fill a hole inside the box of knowledge, using the old
rules. Einstein did something else: he stepped outside the box and made the box bigger.


# 13. Reason 2: deduction can't start (logic needs the rules first)

Logic needs the rules first. LLMs can apply logic if they are given some rules and axioms. Deduction goes
from rules to conclusions, but it cannot create the rules. Give a computer the axioms of geometry, and it
can prove that a triangle's angles add up to 180 degrees, but it will not invent a new geometry. In the
same way, an LLM given Einstein's axioms could probably derive his theory of relativity. Finding the
axioms was the hard part.


# 14. Reason 3: no grounding in the physical world

Before understanding this reason, we first need to understand what an LLM is, what language is, and how
language is different from science and from the reality of the world.

LLMs are just word prediction models. Given an input sequence, they predict the next sequence as output.
What they learn is language, not the physical world and not science itself!

This is a bit difficult to understand, so first let's clarify that language is not equal to science, is
not equal to the physical world, and is not equal to reality. What is language? It is just an interface
for communicating with each other, and also a tool for thinking (as the linguist Noam Chomsky
describes it). We humans explain the world around us using language, in order to communicate with each
other. Unlike LLMs, which can only understand patterns in written text, we humans have multiple senses:
we can see, we can hear, we can smell, we can touch, AND we can understand language. So language is just
a tool. It is not the science or the reality of the world itself, but a tool to explain them. There is a
lot of information in the world around us that cannot be expressed or fully conveyed by language, and it
does not need language to exist.

Imagine a child who has just learned to walk but cannot talk yet. That learning happened without
language. It would be silly to say that the child cannot learn anything because it cannot talk.

Another example is the naming of colors. We humans have the ability to see, and we can see the color
green. The color green is a reality for us humans. In order to communicate with each other, we decided
to call it "green". So the word "green" is just an agreement, not the actual green color we observe with
our biological eyes. Imagine someone who is born, and from the beginning, everyone around them "lies" to
them and says that the name of this specific color is "pink". This new-born human will call it pink while
seeing green. The reality stays the same: the color green stays green, but language taught this child to
call it pink. This means that reality and language are two separate things, and language is just a tool
to explain reality.

So what are these AI models called? They are called "language" models. They are not called smell models
or physics models, but language models, because all they understand is language patterns and nothing
more. And guess what? It happens that we humans have written most of our knowledge and understanding of
the world in the form of natural language (written text). So, by accident, when LLMs learn the language,
they also learn the science, the knowledge and the reality itself! I know it is a bit oversimplified, but
it is a kind of accident: learning language unintentionally teaches you some part of reality too! We
train LLMs to learn language, not the world around us. And the world around us has a lot of useful and
rich information that cannot be captured by language.

What do I mean by "rich"? Imagine you ate a tasty slice of pizza, and you are explaining to your friend
how this pizza tasted. The feeling it gave to the taste buds on your tongue can be expressed in language:
"tasty". If your friend asks you to express it in a richer way, you may say: "tasty, salty and yummy". If
they ask you to express it even more, you would say: "tasty, salty, yummy and flavorful". The problem
here is that even if you explain the taste of that pizza in more detail and with richer language, your
friend still cannot really understand how that pizza "tastes". Even if you explain it for one more hour,
your friend cannot feel how it tastes like you did. Your friend needs to actually go to that pizza shop
and eat the pizza. Explaining in natural language is not equal to eating the pizza. After your friend
"eats" the pizza, they learn how it tastes. Your natural language description of the pizza was just a
tool to communicate with your friend, not the pizza itself!

So why am I giving this example? Because the world around us is very complex, and LLMs cannot learn it.
LLMs cannot feel touch like us humans. LLMs cannot smell like humans. LLMs cannot see colors like us.
LLMs cannot understand the complex and rich information available in the world. They can only
understand language.

After understanding all of this, let's get back to the author's arguments. Zahavy says that since LLMs
have no grounding in the physical world around them, they cannot jump. As his reference, he gives
Einstein's example, which led to General Relativity. Einstein's famous theory of General Relativity
started when he imagined himself in an elevator. To understand it, first a small fact about "weight":
what you feel as your weight is not gravity pulling you; it is the floor pushing back up against your
feet.

- When you stand in a closed elevator on Earth, with no windows, you feel pressed against the floor. If
  you drop a ball, it falls to the floor.
- The opposite side: imagine the same elevator far away in empty space, where there is no gravity, but
  a rocket pulls the elevator upward, faster and faster. You also feel pressed against the floor. If you
  drop a ball, the floor rushes up to meet it, so it looks exactly like the ball is falling. From inside
  the elevator, you cannot tell the two situations apart.
- And the other way round: if the elevator cable on Earth is cut and the elevator falls, you float
  inside it, weightless, exactly like an astronaut in space.

Einstein did not really ride such an elevator; he imagined it in his mind (a "thought experiment"). But
his imagination used real feelings of his physical body that every human knows: the feeling of weight,
of being pushed up in a lift, of the stomach drop when falling. Then he thought: maybe we should rethink
(jump) our understanding of gravity. Maybe gravity and acceleration are the same thing. This means that a
feeling from his senses in the physical world led him to jump. His jump was grounded in physical reality.

But we know that LLMs cannot feel reality like humans, since they do not have biological bodies, and all
they do is learn natural language. That's why LLMs cannot jump like Einstein. An LLM can describe falling
perfectly, but it has never felt weightless, and Einstein's jump started exactly from imagining that
feeling.

An LLM is like a person who has read 100 articles about how to swim, but has never really gone into the
water to swim! It cannot understand how it feels when the cells of your skin touch the water.


# 15. Zahavy's proposed fix: world models

So we know that LLMs learn language, not the world around them. So why haven't humans created AIs or
models that learn the world rather than language?

Actually, we have. There is something called a world model. Unlike LLMs, which predict the next token
given an input text, world models predict the next STATE of the world, given an input environment. LLMs
learn how the text changes given an input text; world models learn how the state of the world changes
given an environment.

Here is an example, so you can understand it better:
- LLM input/output: text → text
  Example: "The ball falls off the …" → (the model predicts) → "edge"
- World model input/output: observation + action → observation (video, 3D)
  Example: [a ball on a table] + push → [the ball falls off] (this happens in a 3D world)

So in this example, if you create a 3D world environment (the observed state) in which the input is a
ball being pushed on a table (the action in the environment), the world model will predict a new state
of this 3D world in which the ball has fallen off the table (the next observed state). Unlike text-only
models, world models capture space, time, object persistence (objects keep existing when you don't see
them), and cause and effect.

Zahavy adds one important point: a model that only watches videos is not enough. A video model can show
an apple falling, but only because apples fall in most of its training videos, not because it
understands gravity. What we need are interactive world models, where the AI can act and ask "what would
happen if…?", for example "what if I cut the elevator cable?". Zahavy calls this a "synthetic
laboratory": a place where an AI could run its own thought experiments, like Einstein did.

One of the important scientists who advocates a lot for world models is Yann LeCun, a Turing Award
winner and one of the fathers of deep learning. He argues that LLMs alone are not enough to reach
human-level intelligence. He has a famous explanation of why we need to learn from the physical world
rather than from language: a four-year-old child has been awake for about 16,000 hours. In that time,
through its eyes alone, the child has taken in about as much data as, or even more than, all the text
used to train the biggest LLMs, and a human would need hundreds of thousands of years to read that text.
And this visual information is much richer than text. His example shows why LLMs are not enough for us
to reach human-level intelligence.

A bit more on world models, for those who are interested. There are two main approaches:
- Generative (observation-level) models: they predict future sensory states directly, such as video
  frames or interactive 3D scenes. Examples include Google DeepMind's Genie 3, World Labs' Marble and
  NVIDIA Cosmos.
- Predictive (latent-space) models: they predict future states in an abstract, compressed space
  (an "embedding space") instead of drawing every pixel. Approaches like JEPA (Joint Embedding
  Predictive Architecture) by Yann LeCun focus on high-level dynamics and physical relationships.


# 16. Summary of what Zahavy says — and an open question

In order to have an AI that can jump, we need something that can do grounded abduction: an abduction
that is rooted in physical grounding. As his reference, Zahavy mentions Einstein's example.

But wait: Einstein is just a single example. Is embodiment (physical grounding) always necessary, or is it
only one of the possible routes to a jump? The authors of the second paper, Balani and Panda, provide a
counterexample.


# 17. Paper 2's response: the Max Planck counterexample

Balani and Panda agree with Zahavy that LLMs can induce and deduce, but cannot make the abductive jump.
But they disagree that the missing ingredient is physical grounding (embodiment). They have a
counterexample: Max Planck, in the year 1900.

At that time, physicists wanted to calculate how much energy a hot object (like a glowing oven) gives off
as light. Classical physics (close to the physics we learn at high school), when applied correctly,
predicted that a hot object emits infinite energy at high frequencies. This was called the "ultraviolet
catastrophe". It was not a measurement error and not a math mistake; it was the correct result of the
classical assumptions. But the measured energy was finite.

Then the jump happened, by Max Planck. Planck jumped to a new axiom: energy is exchanged in small
packets. (Physicists write it as E = hν, "E equals h nu", but you don't need the formula.) This idea later
became the start of quantum physics.

- But was this induction? No. There was no observation or experiment that people could generalize into
  this rule; the confirming experiments came later.
- Was it deduction? No. Deduction from the classical rules is exactly what produced the catastrophe
  (infinite energy).
- Was it grounded abduction? No. Max Planck did not invent this because of any physical experiment or
  feeling. Einstein jumped because of an imagined feeling in an elevator, of falling, but for Max Planck
  the jump came only from a mathematical formula that was very wrong, resulting in infinite energy!
  Planck himself later called his idea "an act of desperation".

So what happened that made this jump? What drove this jump? "The cost of error"! For a scientist like Max
Planck, the infinite value was mathematically far too much, and Planck could not accept it mentally.
Keeping the old rule had become too costly. A small error can be ignored (or patched, like what
scientists did before Einstein), but an infinite one cannot. To get into the details of why this huge
cost led to the jump, read the next section.


# 18. Biological abduction: does being wrong cost anything?

The main reason Max Planck had to jump was the cost of this error. For humans, some errors cost a lot.
But for a machine, does being wrong cost anything?

In order to answer this, we first need to understand a little thermodynamics: coupled and decoupled
systems. A system is thermodynamically coupled if its physical cost grows with its error. (In math
language: the derivative of the cost with respect to the error is positive.) A system is decoupled if its
cost stays the same, no matter how big its error is.

The human brain is coupled. According to the neuroscientist Karl Friston's "free-energy principle", a
prediction error changes the activity between our brain cells (synaptic activity), our attention and our
body's energy use (metabolic demand). As the error grows, the brain works harder, until it revises its
rule.

One example is when you give a speech to a group of 3 people vs. 100 people. When you give a speech to 3
of your close friends and you make a mistake, you usually don't care, since you know nothing bad will
happen with your close friends. But if you make the same mistake in front of 100 people, your heart
starts racing, your blood is pumped with higher pressure, your metabolism burns more energy, and the
activity in your brain increases. This is because your brain goes into a state of uncertainty: you do not
know how 100 strangers will react to your mistake. So the brain is a thermodynamically coupled system.

In thermodynamically coupled systems like our brain, as the error grows and the brain becomes uncertain,
the brain works harder until it revises its rule and reaches a state of certainty. Its uncertainty is
"regulatory": it controls what happens next. In plain words: being wrong makes the brain work harder,
until it changes its mind.

The same thing happened to Max Planck: the infinite value was huge, and it put too much pressure of
uncertainty on Planck's brain. The brain hates uncertainty, so this huge error made his brain work harder,
until it revised the rules (the jump) and came up with a solution (small, discrete packets of energy).
Finally, Planck's brain could reach a state of certainty, and he could take a deep breath.

But what about machines? An LLM, which uses the Transformer architecture, is a decoupled system at
inference time (the time when we use it). The weights of the model are always fixed (frozen) at runtime.
Whether the model is certain or uncertain about a question, it uses the same watts and the same FLOPs
(the same electricity and the same amount of calculation) for a true answer or a wrong answer. (This is
not measured; it simply follows from how the Transformer is built.)

In simpler words: whether an LLM gives a correct answer or a wrong answer, its energy use does not
change. And something else does not change either: its own measure of uncertainty, which is called
"token entropy". A human brain in a state of uncertainty spends more energy (heart racing, higher blood
pressure and metabolism), but an LLM, whether hallucinating or giving a correct answer, uses the same
energy, and its token entropy stays the same. The authors also provide empirical evidence for this.


# 19. Empirical evidence: LLMs are decoupled systems

Is there evidence? The paper cites an experiment by the researcher Ahmed Gamal Eldin (2026); it is not
the authors' own experiment. He did a benchmark on a few LLMs. He gave the LLMs tasks of three different
difficulties:

- A task the LLM was already trained on, so it only needs to answer by simply retrieving information from
  its frozen weights. These are the "Kepler" tasks, which are easy. Example: "A pendulum becomes four
  times longer. What is its new period?"
- A task about things the model was trained on, but where it has to apply logic with new parameters.
  These are the "Newton" tasks. Example: "What if gravity got weaker with distance in a slightly
  different way than Newton said?"
- A third task that was completely beyond its training data, meaning the LLM had no clue about it. These
  are the "Newton OOD" (out of distribution) tasks. Example: physics in an imaginary universe where
  nature's basic constants have different values.

He did this on three Llama models, from 3 billion to 70 billion parameters. For example, the results of
the 70-billion model were as expected: 100% correct on the Kepler tasks, since they were easy, but only
17% correct on the Newton OOD tasks, since these tasks were not in the training data of the LLM and were
very difficult.

If the LLM were a coupled system like the human brain, then when it faced the Newton OOD (difficult)
tasks, it should have become much more uncertain, similar to Max Planck: its token entropy should have
gone up.

By the way, what is token entropy? It is a measure of uncertainty in LLMs, showing how confident and
certain an LLM is about its next answer. Entropy measures how unsure the model is about its next token.
At every step, an LLM does not pick just one next word; it gives a probability to every possible next
word, and entropy measures how spread out these probabilities are:
- After "The capital of France is", the model gives "Paris" 99%. It is very sure, so the entropy is
  almost zero (about 0.06).
- If the model is torn 50/50 between two words, the entropy is higher (about 0.69).
- If it is torn between four words equally, it is higher still (about 1.39).
(The unit is called a "nat". You don't need to remember it; just remember: low = sure, high = unsure.)

The results were interesting: the entropy of the 70-billion model moved by a very small number, from
about 0.09 to about 0.10, and it was statistically flat across all the models. We can say it did not
change at all: whether the model knew the answer or not, its certainty stayed the same.

(A fair note: this main test was small, only six questions per difficulty level. But the author repeated
it with another model, GPT-4o-mini, on 1,000 maths problems per level: accuracy fell from 95% to 62%,
and entropy again almost did not move.)

On the other hand, we know that the compute per token is fixed by the Transformer architecture, so
failing costs nothing extra for the model. Accuracy collapses, and the model does not even notice that it
is failing. That is why the authors named their paper "LLMs Don't Pay for the Jump"!


# 20. A question people usually ask me: "But LLMs get feedback, don't they?"

"An LLM can get feedback and fix itself, for example during training with reinforcement learning. So why
does flat entropy matter?"

Feedback comes from outside: from the dataset's correct answers in training, from human raters in RLHF
(reinforcement learning from human feedback), or from a user saying "that's wrong". Someone who already
knows the truth supplies the signal. Entropy measures what the model knows by itself, before any
feedback, and it shows that the model cannot tell its wrong answers from its right ones.

In real discovery, nobody has the answer key: in 1900, no one could tell Planck the right answer. The
pressure to change must come from inside, and this is the "coupling" that Paper 2 asks for. (Even in
training, one step costs the same compute whether the error is big or small; only the size of the update
changes. But Paper 2's own claim is about inference, with frozen weights.)

In one sentence: feedback means someone tells you that you are wrong; the authors want a model that
feels by itself that it is wrong.


# 21. The fix proposed by Balani and Panda: making the model "pay"

While Zahavy's proposal was "physically grounded" abduction, the authors of the second paper propose
"cost-coupled" abduction. They hypothesize that a system can create a new axiom only if an internal cost
grows with its error on a specific result, until the rule is revised.

So when a model makes a prediction and observes an error at inference time, its energy cost goes up ↑,
and its weights get revised. It is like pain after a wrong move: the bigger the error, the bigger the
energy bill, until the model updates itself. This could be possible with neuromorphic (brain-like)
hardware, where prediction errors keep updating the weights during inference, and each update costs
more energy for a larger error.

(To be fair: this is a proposal. Nobody has built and tested such a system yet. But the authors make
their idea testable: in a coupled system, the cost signal should follow the accuracy. If it stays flat,
their idea is wrong.)


# 22. Wrap-up

So both papers say the same thing: LLMs can't jump. But they give different reasons and different
solutions.

For Zahavy, the missing part is sensory grounding; for Balani and Panda, it is a cost of error. Zahavy
proposes interactive world models; Balani and Panda propose coupled architectures. (Zahavy's evidence is
historical; Balani and Panda add a cited entropy study.) Both agree: today's LLMs can induce and deduce,
but they cannot abduce.

Back to our box of knowledge: today's LLMs can fill the empty spaces inside the box, using the rules and
data we already have. But according to both papers, they cannot think outside of the box and make the box
bigger. That is the jump.

| | Zahavy (Paper 1) | Balani and Panda (Paper 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Main example | Einstein, General Relativity | Planck, energy packets |
| What is missing | grounding in the physical world | a cost for being wrong |
| Proposed fix | interactive world models | cost-coupled (brain-like) systems |
| Evidence | history | history + a cited entropy study |

Maybe the two ideas even fit together. Grounding could tell a model what the world is like, and a cost
for being wrong could tell it when it is wrong enough to change its mind. A future AI scientist may need
both.
